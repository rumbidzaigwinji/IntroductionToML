Module 1: Foundations (Slides 1-7)

Slides covered: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7

Teaching Narrative

Start by reframing computation from static rule-writing to pattern learning. In traditional programming, humans specify every rule. In ML, we provide examples and let the model infer useful decision structure.

Then position the hierarchy clearly: AI is the broad umbrella, ML is a subset of AI, and Deep Learning is a subset of ML that uses multi-layer neural networks. This gives students a clean mental map before technical details begin.

Key Concepts And Formulas

\[ \text{Traditional Programming: Rules + Data } \to \text{ Answers} \]
\[ \text{Machine Learning: Data + Answers } \to \text{ Learned Rules (Model)} \]
\[ \text{AI} \supset \text{ML} \supset \text{DL} \]
\[ \text{RL objective: maximize } \sum_{t} R_t \]

Slide Cues

  • Slide 2: emphasize why manual rule design fails for complex patterns.
  • Slide 3: define AI, ML, DL with one concrete example each.
  • Slide 4: compare supervised vs unsupervised vs reinforcement learning.
  • Slide 5: explain supervised samples as paired inputs and labels.
  • Slide 6: unsupervised learning discovers structure without labels.
  • Slide 7: in RL, actions are learned through rewards and penalties.

Classroom Prompts

  • Ask: "Is customer segmentation supervised or unsupervised? Why?"
  • Ask: "What feedback signal exists in reinforcement learning?"

Module 2: Supervised Learning (Slides 8-13)

Slides covered: 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13

Teaching Narrative

Teach this module by first separating output types: regression predicts a continuous value, classification predicts a discrete label. This single distinction removes most beginner confusion.

Next, bridge deterministic label prediction with probabilistic thinking. Show that many classifiers estimate class probabilities first, then apply a decision threshold.

Key Concepts And Formulas

\[ \hat{y} = f(x) \]
\[ \hat{y} = wx + b \]
\[ \text{error}_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i \]
\[ P(y=1 \mid x) \]
\[ \hat{y}=1 \quad \text{if} \quad P(y=1\mid x) \ge 0.5 \]

Slide Cues

  • Slide 8: anchor regression with local examples like demand forecasting.
  • Slide 9: explain the role of slope \(w\) and intercept \(b\).
  • Slide 10: connect per-sample error to training objective later.
  • Slide 11: define classification as label prediction from finite classes.
  • Slide 12: contrast binary and multiclass settings.
  • Slide 13: discuss threshold choice as a business decision.

Classroom Prompts

  • Ask: "Is malaria risk prediction regression or classification?"
  • Ask: "What changes when we move threshold from 0.5 to 0.8?"

Module 3: Training Math (Slides 14-18)

Slides covered: 14, 15, 16, 17, 18

Teaching Narrative

This module formalizes the ML pipeline mathematically. Students should leave this section able to read symbolic notation without fear: dataset, prediction function, loss, objective, and parameter updates.

Keep the intuition explicit: we search parameter space to reduce average error over the training data.

Key Concepts And Formulas

\[ D=\{(x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2),\dots,(x_n,y_n)\} \]
\[ X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}, \quad y \in \mathbb{R}^{n} \]
\[ \hat{y}=f_{\theta}(x) \]
\[ J(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L\left(y_i,f_{\theta}(x_i)\right) \]
\[ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \nabla_{\theta}J(\theta) \]

Slide Cues

  • Slide 14: explain \(n\) (rows/examples) and \(d\) (features/columns).
  • Slide 15: \(\theta\) are learnable parameters, not fixed constants.
  • Slide 16: loss is a per-example penalty; lower is better.
  • Slide 17: objective is average loss across all training examples.
  • Slide 18: gradient points uphill; update goes downhill.

Classroom Prompts

  • Ask: "What happens if learning rate \(\alpha\) is too large?"
  • Ask: "Why do we average the loss over \(n\) examples?"

Module 4: Evaluation Metrics (Slides 19-29)

Slides covered: 19 through 29

Teaching Narrative

Reinforce the central idea: training success does not equal deployment usefulness. Evaluation answers whether the model generalizes, what mistakes it makes, and whether those mistakes are acceptable for the task.

For regression, quantify numeric prediction error. For classification, inspect confusion patterns and trade-offs between precision and recall.

Regression Metrics

\[ \text{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_i-\hat{y}_i| \]
\[ \text{MSE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2 \]
\[ \text{RMSE}=\sqrt{\text{MSE}} \]

Classification Metrics

Confusion terms: TP, TN, FP, FN

\[ \text{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN} \]
\[ \text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP} \]
\[ \text{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} \]
\[ F_1=2\cdot\frac{\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}} \]

Slide Cues

  • Slides 20-23: explain why RMSE penalizes large misses heavily.
  • Slide 25: draw the confusion matrix and label each cell aloud.
  • Slide 26: show why accuracy fails under class imbalance.
  • Slides 27-28: connect precision/recall to FP/FN business cost.
  • Slide 29: present F1 as a balanced compromise metric.

Classroom Prompts

  • Ask: "In disease screening, which is worse: FP or FN?"
  • Ask: "When would you prefer MAE over RMSE?"

Module 5: Data Cleaning And Scaling (Slides 30-32)

Slides covered: 30, 31, 32

Teaching Narrative

Stress that preprocessing is not optional. Data quality defines the ceiling of model quality. Poorly treated missing values or inconsistent scales can dominate model behavior more than algorithm choice.

Key Formulas

\[ z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} \]
\[ x'=\frac{x-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}} \]

Slide Cues

  • Slide 30: list common data quality problems before modeling begins.
  • Slide 31: compare row deletion, mean/median/mode imputation, model-based imputation.
  • Slide 32: explain why scaling matters for KNN, K-Means, logistic regression, neural nets.

Classroom Prompts

  • Ask: "When might median be better than mean for imputation?"
  • Ask: "Why is scaling less critical for tree-based methods?"

Module 6: Workflow And Model Validation (Slides 33-35)

Slides covered: 33, 34, 35

Teaching Narrative

Present ML as a repeatable lifecycle, not a one-shot model fit. Clean split discipline is essential for honest performance estimation.

Workflow And Validation

\[ \text{Data} \to \text{Clean} \to \text{Split} \to \text{Train} \to \text{Evaluate} \to \text{Improve} \]
  • Train set: learn parameters.
  • Validation set: tune model choices and hyperparameters.
  • Test set: final unbiased estimate of generalization.

Generalization Patterns

  • Underfitting: weak train and weak test performance.
  • Good fit: strong train and strong test performance.
  • Overfitting: strong train but weak test performance.

Classroom Prompts

  • Ask: "Why must we avoid repeated tuning on the test set?"
  • Ask: "What is one practical sign of overfitting in metrics?"

Module 7: Broader AI Concepts (Slides 36-39)

Slides covered: 36, 37, 38, 39

Teaching Narrative

Expand student perspective beyond tabular supervised learning. Show that core ML principles transfer to vision, language, recommendation, and generative systems, even though data modalities and model architectures differ.

Domain Frames

  • Computer Vision: classification, detection, segmentation, recognition.
  • NLP: text classification, translation, summarization, question answering.
  • Recommendation: predict user-item relevance.
  • Generative AI: create new text, images, audio, code, video.
\[ \text{Recommendation: User Behavior + Item Information} \to \text{Recommendation} \]

Classroom Prompts

  • Ask: "What data do we need for a music recommender?"
  • Ask: "How is generative AI different from predictive classification?"

Module 8: Practical Notebook Roadmap (Slides 40-42)

Slides covered: 40, 41, 42

Teaching Narrative

This section transitions theory to practice. Students should execute a full modeling loop: inspect data, preprocess, train, evaluate, and interpret. Encourage them to explain every result, not only pass automated checks.

Practical Workflow Steps

  1. Load and inspect dataset structure.
  2. Handle missing values and categorical variables.
  3. Split data correctly.
  4. Train baseline and improved models.
  5. Evaluate with task-appropriate metrics.
  6. Produce challenge submission probabilities in required format.

Practical Metrics To Revisit

  • Regression: MAE, RMSE, \(R^2\).
  • Classification: Precision, Recall, F1, ROC-AUC, PR-AUC.
  • Competition ranking: PR-AUC first, then F1, then lower log loss.
